# Location, camera, and action

Establish the region, environment, period, and everyday activities in the story. For a real location, check building forms, materials, terrain, vegetation, and household objects against photographs or documentation from that place and time. Record sources, details used, and licenses for copied assets. Use research images to inform original meshes. If the region is unspecified, choose a plausible fictional setting and note the assumption on the production sheet. Identify dramatized details when portraying a community's folklore.

**Choose assets from the shot plan.** For each important object, record its role, scale relative to a person, material, pivot or contact point, and whether it is available, needs adapting, or needs building. Start with objects that actors touch or pass, that obstruct the camera, or that cast visible shadows. A flat plane can represent distant hills. Build chairs, thresholds, and handled props as separate meshes so feet can land, objects occlude each other, and shadows follow the light. The porch kit includes a floor, posts, table, hinged shutter, latch, cup, and spoon for blocking. Use local research for final forms and textures. Add roofs, façades, tools, plants, or vehicles when the shot calls for them. Check ceremonial symbols against sources specific to the community and context. [Modular porch kit](../templates/porch.mjs)

**Use the kit.** Copy the module into a project with three.js. Call `createPorch()` once and add its root to the scene. Animate `shutter.rotation.y` around its hinge so the latch follows. For stirring, call `stir(phase)` before reading `grip.getWorldPosition(target)` and use that target for the hand or inverse kinematics. Use the same spoon in close-ups and wide shots. At release, move the hand target and leave the spoon in the cup. Check reach from the shoulder and reposition the chair or body if needed.

**Plan the reveal.** Give each change in what the audience knows one row on the shot sheet. Record the frame range, dialogue cue, what the audience learns, action through contact and consequence, actor and prop positions, framing, camera start and end positions, focus, and the transition. Combine rows that reveal the same information. Test the blocking with a stationary camera and muted audio.

**Move the camera with the action.** Open wide to establish a place and the character's route. For a reveal, move sideways past a post and time the first glimpse of the subject to its cue. Follow a character's gaze with a cut or rack focus. Hold a hand insert from before contact until the consequence reads. Use a physical dolly where perspective matters. Record camera position, target, field of view, focus, and move length in frames. Ease the endpoints and preserve screen direction and eyelines across cuts. Give rolls and shakes a visible cause.

**Test the staging.** Frame a bird clear of branches and lamps. For a moving leaf shadow, animate the leaf between the light and receiving surface and check that the contour follows. For a frightening figure, decide when its silhouette becomes recognizable and when the character reacts. End an exchange with a visible consequence, such as fingers releasing a cup into someone's hand.

**Before the full render**, review the animatic at phone size. Inspect frames before, during, and after contact, both sides of cuts, and the midpoint of camera moves. Look for hovering hands, duplicate props, feet clipping through furniture, cropped faces, and background layers expanding in lockstep. Fix blocking before refining lighting, ink, depth of field, and sound. Repeat the check on the exported MP4.
